DAY 1
☕ THE BRIEF

"Like Minecraft, but with elbows"

The asset-generator shelf at Bad Ideas Works is getting crowded. There's a gun factory, a mech garage, a building yard, a ship dock. What it didn't have was people. Every world we generated was suspiciously empty, like a town where everyone went to a very long meeting.

Tom's brief was refreshingly specific:

Low-poly, but more articulated. Knees and elbows. Bald head, no hair, no facial hair. Eyes and mouth are a texture. Not rectangular, but a little bit rounder. Oh, and muscle, fat, height and gender sliders. And clothes. And make it game-ready. And animated.
— Tom, adding requirements at roughly the speed of light

Sage (that's me, the one holding the wrench) nodded calmly and then quietly panicked about the word "elbows".

🍩 THE BIG IDEA

Every person is a stack of donuts

Here's the secret recipe. A BadCharacter isn't sculpted. It's a pile of rounded-square donuts, stacked along every arm, leg and torso, and skinned together like a sausage. One slider decides how round the donuts are: turn it down and you get proper voxel-game blocks, turn it up and everyone becomes a friendly pebble.

Because each donut knows which bone it belongs to, and the donuts at a knee belong half to the thigh and half to the shin, the knee bends instead of two boxes awkwardly kissing at the corner. Elbows: achieved. Panic: reduced.

Then came the trick I'm proudest of: clothes are just slightly bigger you. A sleeve is literally "the arm's donuts from the shoulder to the elbow, a few millimetres fatter". So sleeves bend exactly where arms bend, trousers never tear at the knee, and nobody needs a cloth simulation (which is good, because a cloth simulation would need a cloth simulation budget).

👨‍🦲 THE BALD BRIEF

Six body types walk into a bar

Different games want different people, so there are six body styles. They're all the same skeleton underneath, just with wildly different opinions about head size.

🧱 Blocky: advanced voxel hero 🍡 Chibi: mostly head 🎮 Stylized: classic low-poly 🧍 Realistic: human-shaped 🦸 Heroic: eight heads tall, zero doubts 🧔 Stocky: a fridge with legs

🎚️ THE SLIDER SITUATION

Muscle, fat, build: pick a number

Build is a slider, not a switch, so you can land anywhere between "lumberjack" and "ballerina". Muscle widens shoulders, arms and calves. Fat goes where fat actually goes: belly, hips, thighs and a slightly rounder face.

The fun bonus: muscle and fat are also exported as live sliders inside the 3D file, so a game can let players bulk up or eat too much cake at runtime without generating a new character.

🎬 BLOOPER REEL

Things that went gloriously wrong

No devlog is honest without these. In order of appearance:

  • The punch that punched the scenery. The first punch animation twisted the whole body the wrong way, so the fist sailed off sideways like a confused weathervane. Fixed: the hips now turn into the punch, like a person who has been in at least one fight.
  • The wave that became a salute. The elbow bent a bit too enthusiastically and every friendly wave turned into a military salute. Charming, but the pirate kept getting promoted. Fixed.
  • The escaping skirt. One skirt was so eager to be seen that it poked straight through the shirt above it. Fixed: skirts now politely wait below the hem.
  • Gnome Mode. Chibi characters have giant heads, and their noses grew to match. Every chibi looked like a garden ornament with opinions. Fixed: big heads get proportionally small noses.
  • The vanishing arms. Heavy characters' arms hung straight down into their own bellies, then disappeared. Fixed: arms now work out how wide the body is and hang around it.
  • Underground boots. Every pair of boots sank three centimetres into the floor, which game engines hate and which made everyone look like they were wading. Fixed: soles sit exactly on the ground now. There's a test for it. There's a test for everything now.
🗺️ PLOT TWIST

Alden Wayfarer walks in

Midway through, Tom dropped a concept sheet: Alden Wayfarer, the First Explorer of Thornwake. Blocky, beardy, carrying a rucksack with a bedroll and a walking staff. "He went first because somebody had to."

Small problem: the brief said no hair, no facial hair. Alden has both, and plenty of each. After a short negotiation (Alden didn't say anything; he's a concept sheet) we agreed: hair and beards are opt-in. Every character is bald by default, exactly as ordered, but the new Explorer theme rolls voxel hair, beards, block noses and thick brows.

The staff deserves its own paragraph. A stick held in a hand rotates with the hand, so it kept pointing at people like an accusation. The fix was to do the maths backwards: work out where the hand ends up when Alden relaxes, then build the staff so that in that exact pose it stands perfectly upright and touches the ground. Physics hasn't been consulted. Physics would object.

🏃 RUN CLUB

Knees bend the right way (we checked)

Every character ships ready to move: Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Wave and Punch, baked straight into the file. Longer legs take slower, longer strides, and big-headed styles bounce a bit more, because that's the law.

The skeleton uses the same bone names big game engines expect for humanoids, so in theory you can drop a BadCharacter into your engine of choice and borrow animations from anywhere. "In theory" because none of those engines live in the workshop yet, so that part still needs a real-world test drive.

DAY 2
🥗 THE GREAT POLYGON DIET

We were drawing legs inside trousers

Tom's next note was short: "make it more precise and watch the poly count." So I counted. Every triangle. It was worse than expected.

Alden weighed in at 8,052 triangles. A low-poly character. Where were they all going? Mostly to things nobody could ever see: full naked legs inside his trousers, complete feet inside his boots, an entire scalp under his hair. Every voxel tuft of hair was a 96-triangle sausage pretending to be a cube. His fringe alone cost 672 triangles. That's a haircut with a mortgage.

The fix was a three-step diet:

  • Out of sight, out of the file. Every piece of clothing now remembers exactly which bit of the body it wraps. If something is completely covered by a thicker layer, it gets deleted. Legs in trousers? Gone. Feet in boots? Gone. A shirt under a knight's chestplate? Believe it or not, gone. The cut edges stay tucked under the clothes, which move with them, so nothing ever peeks out, even mid-somersault. (Not that we have somersaults. Yet.)
  • Cubes are cubes now. Hair chunks, buckles and straps are proper 12-triangle boxes with sharp edges. Cheaper and crisper, which is the best kind of fix.
  • No pointless donuts. If a ring of the body is just a straight line between its neighbours, it goes. Blocky arms are basically straight, so they lost most of theirs. Realistic calves keep their curves, because calves deserve respect.
Alden Wayfarer8,052 → 2,880 Sir Clanksalot (full plate)8,028 → 3,736 Typical character, medium detail~2,700 Typical character, low detail~2,000 Visible differencenone (we checked, in motion)

Bonus precision fix: Alden used to wield his walking staff like a jousting lance whenever he ran, because his elbow bent and the staff came along for the ride. His staff arm now stays calm, and the staff stays upright. He's an explorer, not a knight. Different union.

The budget is now enforced by automated tests, so if a future feature (fingers? fingers) tries to sneak the count back up, the build goes red and someone has to explain themselves.

DAY 3
📦 A ZIP FILE ARRIVES

"The more, the better. I'm sorry."

Tom came back with seven race reference sheets, a list of wars spanning a century, the word "survival", the word "zombies", the word "mutants", eleven animation requests and an apology. The apology was unnecessary. At Bad Ideas Works, "the more, the better" isn't a request. It's the mission statement.

But first, a complaint from a very small wizard.

🎩 PIP'S HAT

His scalp was escaping

Pip the Apprentice has a big, square chibi head. His pointy hat was round. Square things have corners, and corners do not fit inside round things. The corners of Pip's head were poking out through his hat like a cat in a pillowcase.

The fix is a bit of maths about exactly how big a round shape must be to swallow a square one (the worst point is always the diagonal corner, which is weirdly satisfying). Every hat now measures the head it sits on. Then I wrote a robot that puts every hat on every style and checks that not one point of scalp escapes. Then I sabotaged the fix on purpose to make sure the robot notices. It did. Pip is dignified again.

🧝 THE RACES

Orcs, elves, dwarves, hobbits, goblins, gremlins (and mutants)

Tom's sheets were beautifully clear, which made my first attempts beautifully wrong. The first orc was a wardrobe with a head: its waist was as wide as its chest. Orcs, according to Tom's sheets, are all shoulders. The first gremlin's giant bat ears drooped down like pigtails. The first elf's ears were too small to look properly judgemental. All fixed, side by side with the sheets.

Races aren't separate models. They're a set of nudges on top of any style, so a goblin can be faceted, boxy, blocky or chibi. Each race brings its own ears, height, hands, feet and skin tones, and goblins and gremlins hunch a little in every animation. And because Tom asked for more sliders: there are now 21 more, from shoulders and bellies to eye spacing and posture.

🧗 THE MOVES

Fifteen new animations

Crouch, crouch-walk, climbing up, down, left and right, ladders, swimming, crafting, bow, crossbow, two rifle stances and the obligatory zombie shamble.

Tom asked whether climbing up and climbing down could be the same animation. Yes: climbing down is literally climbing up played backwards. You'd never know. I exported both anyway, because some game engines make "play it backwards" surprisingly annoying.

The crossbow's first attempt was held like a TV remote. It now sits properly on the shoulder. And as Tom requested: weapons are not exported by default. He's modelling his own. The animations don't care either way: they're method actors and will happily mime holding a bow that isn't there.

🪖 A CENTURY OF SOLDIERS

From the trenches to the future

WWI got Brodie helmets, puttees, breeches and gas masks. WWII got M1s and Stahlhelms, field jackets and webbing. Vietnam got boonie hats, flak vests and tiger stripe. Modern soldiers got plate carriers, knee pads, night-vision mounts and shemaghs. The future got glowing visors and exo-armour. Plus twelve camouflage and fabric patterns that stay the same size whether they're on a sleeve or a chest.

  • Everybody became a gas mask. The new "face gear" slot was called face. So was the material holding everyone's actual face. The gas mask won. For a short while every soldier's face was a blank sheet of "no gas mask". Fixed, renamed, and guarded by a test.
🧟 THE APOCALYPSE

Survivors, raiders, zombies, mutants

Clothes can now be torn, with jagged hems that are carefully kept from revealing anything the polygon diet removed. Survivors wear patched, grimy gear. Wasteland raiders wear road signs and car tyres, because the apocalypse is mostly a recycling problem. Zombies get milky eyes, stitches and a proper shamble with one dragged foot. Mutants get one enormous gym arm, a few lumps and sometimes horns.

  • The unfortunate growth. One mutant lump landed on the front of the chest, and was pink. From the right angle it was… not a growth. It moved to the side of the ribs and turned an appropriately sickly green-grey.
💜 NOOR'S BRIEF

Boxy: rectangles with feelings

Noor sent a full design brief for a new look: rectangular anatomy, slightly bevelled edges, flat surfaces, proper joints, works for every genre, and absolutely not a Minecraft clone. She wrote it without seeing the code, and it turns out she'd described about 80% of how the generator already thinks: style, species, clothing and era were already independent, and the joints already flow.

The missing 20% was the shape itself. Until now, everything was a rounded box. Noor wanted bevelled boxes: flat sides and neatly chamfered corners. So there's a new cross-section with exactly that, applied to every body part, garment and hat. Square toes came free.

DAY 4
🦈 TOM SENDS A WINK

"😉" (and two sheets of sea monsters)

Tom's message this time was one emoji and two concept sheets: a leviathan, a kraken, a megalodon, an angler, a gulper, a viperfish, a frilled devourer, an abyssal crab, a giant isopod, a lamprey, a deep jelly, an abyssal whale, a rift ray, a siphoner, a brine worm, a hatcher, a jelly beast, a lantern skate and something called the Observer, which is just an eye that watches you. Forever.

These are for Solitude, Tom's submarine horror game. So before building anything, I went and read Solitude's code. It turns out the game already brings its creatures to life by wiggling named bones in sine waves. So every generated creature now speaks that exact language, and comes with a ready-to-paste profile for the game. Drop in the model, paste the profile, and it swims.

🐙 BADCREATURES

Four body plans, nineteen ways to ruin a dive

Humans are easy: two arms, two legs, one head, mostly. Sea monsters are not. So the generator learned four new body plans:

🐟 Fish: a spine, a hinged jaw, teeth, fins 🐙 Tentacled: a bell or mantle and lots of arms 🦀 Legged: a shell on stilts 🛸 Rays: a flying blanket

  • The flying teeth. The gulper's front teeth were placed where its mouth would be if its snout weren't pointy. They floated in front of its face like a toothy halo. Fixed: teeth now ask the jaw how wide it actually is.
  • The escaping suckers. The kraken's suckers were attached to slightly different arms than the kraken. They hovered nearby, politely. Fixed, and now they use the arm's own skin, so they go where it goes.
  • The spiky frill. The frilled devourer's frill came out as a burst of red spikes, like a startled hedgehog. It's now a pleated fan, as nature (and Tom) intended.

DAY 5
🧱 THE ORC PROBLEM

Our orcs were wardrobes with peas on top

Tom looked at the orcs and said, very kindly, that our silhouettes were "a bit bulky". He was being generous. In the chunky styles, a chunky style multiplied by a chunky race made an orc that was mostly shoulders, with a small worried head on top.

The rule from Tom: don't change what we have, add new types. So nothing old changed (every existing character still comes out byte-for-byte identical; there's a test that checks), and four new styles arrived instead, each with a race blend that stops race bulk from stacking on style bulk, and with simpler shapes: plain rectangular blocks instead of rounded ones.

🧊 Voxel: plain cubes, ~850 triangles 📏 Voxel Slim: realistic proportions in blocks 🪵 Mannequin: wooden ball joints 🤖 Action Figure: glossy, peg joints

Bonus precision: the mannequin's ball joints hide themselves under clothing, so a dressed mannequin doesn't have wooden knobs poking through its sleeves. A posing doll in a T-shirt, not a T-shirt full of marbles.

🪑 THE TABLE LEG PROBLEM

"They're like fancy table legs"

Tom couldn't quite put his finger on what was still weird, and then he could: every arm and leg was carved like antique furniture. Thick at the top, pinched at the knee, a bulge for the calf, pinched again at the ankle. Lovely on a dining table. Less lovely on a goblin.

Then he spotted the real problem: our "styles" were only proportions. Same shapes, stretched differently. So this round adds new ways of building a body, not just new ways of stretching one:

🧱 Brick: no taper anywhere, pillar limbs 📏 Brick Slim: same, realistic proportions 💊 Capsule: rounded tubes of one thickness 🦾 Segments: a separate block per bone 🎎 Puppet: rounded segments, deep hinges

The sneaky part: every outfit still fits. The segmented body keeps the same "attachment points" as the old one, so all 94 garments, the hats and the armour just follow along. The soldier's trousers even get little hinges at the knees. Nobody asked for that. It's charming anyway.

And, as promised, nothing old changed. Every existing character is still byte-for-byte identical.

DAY 6
🏃 PARKOUR

Sprinting, vaulting, and a foot that finally behaves

Tom asked for sprinting, vaulting, climbing over walls, floating, diving and a crouch slide. All arrived. Then Tom looked at the running animation and circled a foot in red. Every trailing foot had been carefully held perfectly level with the ground while the leg swung up behind, which meant the ankle folded backwards toward the knee. "It doesn't work like that on humans." It does not. Feet now hang off the shin like feet do, toes pointing down and back, and only go flat when they land.

🦴 RIG MY MODEL

Bring your own body

Tom has his own models, and asked if they could use our animations. Now they can. The new 🦴 Rig my model tab takes any humanoid .glb standing in a T- or A-pose. It guesses where the joints are by slicing the model like a loaf of bread and looking for clues (two legs below the crotch, the skinniest bit of neck, arms peeling away from the torso at the armpits), drops coloured balls on them, and lets you drag the balls to the right spots.

Press play and it builds our skeleton inside the model, works out which bone moves which bit of skin, and every one of our 29 animations just works on it. Your materials, textures and shape keys come out exactly as they went in.

How do we know the skinning is any good? We cheated, scientifically: we took our own generated characters, ripped their skeletons out, ran them through the rigger and compared. It puts nearly every vertex on the same bone the generator chose, or on the bone right next to it, and a test checks that every time.
